Efficacy and Safety of Esketamine for Supplemental Analgesia During Elective Cesarean Delivery

A Randomized Clinical Trial

Li-Li Xu, MD; Chun Wang, MD; Chun-Mei Deng, MD; Shao-Bing Dai, MD; Qun Zhou, MD; Yong-Bao Peng, MD; Hong-Yan Shou, MD; Yin-Qiu Han, MD; Jing Yu, MD; Chao-Hui Liu, MD; Feng Xia, MD; Su-Qin Zhang, MD; Dong-Xin Wang, MD; Xin-Zhong Chen, MD

JAMA Network Open. 2023;6(4):e239321 · doi:10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2023.9321 · Published April 21, 2023 · ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04548973

Abstract

Importance Epidural anesthesia is a primary choice for cesarean delivery, but supplemental analgesics are often required to relieve pain during uterine traction.

Objective To investigate the sedative and analgesic effects of intravenous esketamine administered before childbirth via cesarean delivery with the patient under epidural anesthesia.

Design, Setting, and Participants This multicenter, double-blind randomized clinical trial assessed 903 women 18 years or older who had full-term single pregnancy and were scheduled for elective cesarean delivery with epidural anesthesia in 5 medical centers in China from September 18, 2021, to September 20, 2022.

Intervention Patients were randomized to receive intravenous injection of 0.25 mg/kg of esketamine or placebo before incision.

Main Outcomes and Measures The coprimary outcomes included scores on the numeric rating scale of pain (an 11-point scale, with 0 indicating no pain and 10 indicating the worst pain; a difference of 1.65 points was clinically meaningful) and Ramsay Sedation Scale (a 6-point scale, with 1 indicating restlessness and 6 indicating deep sleep without response; a difference of 2 points was clinically meaningful) immediately after fetal delivery. Secondary outcomes included neonatal Apgar score assessed at 1 and 5 minutes after birth.

Results A total of 600 women (mean [SD] age, 30.7 [4.3] years) were enrolled and randomized; all were included in the intention-to-treat analysis. Immediately after fetal delivery, the score on the numeric rating scale of pain was lower with esketamine (median [IQR], 0 [0-1]) than with placebo (median [IQR], 0 [0-2]; median difference, 0; 95% CI, 0-0; P = .001), but the difference was not clinically important. The Ramsay Sedation Scale scores were higher (sedation deeper) with esketamine (median [IQR], 4 [3-4]) than with placebo (median [IQR], 2 [2-2]; median difference, 2; 95% CI, 2-2; P < .001). The neonatal Apgar scores did not differ between the 2 groups at 1 minute and at 5 minutes. Transient neurologic or mental symptoms were more common in patients given esketamine (97.7% [293 of 300]) than in those given placebo (4.7% [14 of 300]; P < .001).

Conclusions and Relevance For women undergoing cesarean delivery under epidural anesthesia, a subanesthetic dose of esketamine administered before incision produced transient analgesia and sedation but did not induce significant neonatal depression. Mental symptoms and nystagmus were common but transient. Indications and the optimal dose of esketamine in this patient population need further clarification, but study should be limited to those who require supplemental analgesia.

Key Points

Question Is intravenous esketamine safe and effective before childbirth during cesarean delivery with epidural anesthesia?

Findings In this multicenter, double-blind, randomized clinical trial of 600 women undergoing cesarean delivery with epidural anesthesia, a subanesthetic dose of esketamine administered before incision produced transient analgesia and sedation and easily passed through the placental barrier but did not induce significant neonatal depression. Mental symptoms and nystagmus were common but transient.

Meaning Indications and the optimal dose of esketamine in this patient population need further clarification, but study should be limited to those who require supplemental analgesia.

Introduction

Epidural anesthesia is a widely used technique for cesarean delivery. It is the first-choice method when labor epidural analgesia is converted to cesarean delivery anesthesia and when it is critical to avoid hypotension during induction of neuraxial blockage. However, because of incomplete block of the splanchnic nerve, some parturients experience discomfort or pain during exploration of the uterus, pulling peritoneum, and fetal delivery.

The referred pain not only brings painful memories and mental stress to parturients but also affects the conduct of the surgical procedure; in some cases, change to general anesthesia is required. Therefore, it is often necessary to administer supplemental analgesics during cesarean delivery with epidural anesthesia. Opioids (such as fentanyl and sufentanil) and dexmedetomidine have been administered for this purpose; however, adverse effects, including respiratory depression, itching, and hypotension, limit their widespread use.

Esketamine, a dextrorotatory isomer of ketamine, is 2 times more potent than ketamine in hypnotic and analgesic effect and less likely to produce psychiatric adverse reactions. In a preclinical study of pregnant sheep, Strümper et al verified that esketamine has similar effects on uterine perfusion and has limited effects on maternal and fetal hemodynamics and respiration when compared with racemic ketamine; thus, it might be an appropriate analgesic in the obstetric setting.

In a clinical study of women undergoing cesarean delivery, Unlugenc et al found that adding esketamine (0.05 mg/kg) to intrathecal plain bupivacaine (10 mg) resulted in rapid onset of sensory and motor block and enhanced segmental spread of spinal block but did not prolong the duration of spinal anesthesia. Suppa et al reported that, for women after cesarean delivery, preventive esketamine administration had analgesic effects and was safe.

However, the efficacy and safety of intravenous esketamine before childbirth are still unclear. We therefore investigated the sedative and analgesic effects of intravenous esketamine before childbirth during cesarean delivery with epidural anesthesia. We also observed placental transfer as well as the effects on maternal and fetal outcomes.

Methods

Study Design and Patient Enrollment

This double-blinded, placebo-controlled randomized clinical trial assessed 903 parturients from 5 medical centers for eligibility. The trial protocol was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Women’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov on September 16, 2021, and patients were enrolled between September 18, 2021, and September 20, 2022. All participants gave written informed consent. The formal trial protocol and statistical analysis plan are provided as Supplement 1. There were no important changes after trial commencement. This report follows the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guideline for randomized trials.

We enrolled women 18 years or older who had a full-term single pregnancy and regular prenatal examination and were scheduled for elective cesarean delivery with epidural anesthesia. The exclusion criteria included body mass index of 27 or greater; previous mental illness, central nervous system disease, liver disease, abnormal kidney function, abnormal heart and lung function, diabetes, or American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification III or above; severe obstetric complications; stillbirth or neonatal malformation; and contraindications to epidural anesthesia.

2

Randomization and Blinding

Random allocations were generated using SPSS software, version 22.0 (IBM Corp) in a 1:1 ratio. Assignments were concealed in sequentially numbered opaque envelopes. Before anesthesia, study coordinators who otherwise were not involved in the trial opened the envelopes according to the recruitment sequence, prepared esketamine or an identical-appearing saline placebo, and gave the study drugs to the attending anesthesiologists.

All patients, anesthesiologists, other health care team members, and investigators who were responsible for data collection and follow-ups were blinded to group allocation.

Anesthesia Management and Intervention

No premedication was administered. In the operating room, heart rate and oxygen saturation were monitored continuously, and blood pressure was noninvasively measured every 5 minutes. Epidural catheterization was performed with parturients in the left lateral decubitus position, using a 16-G Tuohy needle in the L2-L3 intervertebral space. After confirmation of the epidural space by the loss of resistance to air method, a catheter was inserted 3 to 4 cm in a cephalad direction.

A test dose of 5 mL of 1.5% lidocaine was given. After a 5-minute observation, 10 mL of 0.75% ropivacaine was given, followed by an infusion of 0.75% ropivacaine at 3 to 5 mL/h. The upper sensory block was targeted between the T4 and T6 levels. Study drugs were administered when the targeted upper sensory block level was achieved (approximately 2 minutes before incision). Specifically, 0.25 mg/kg of esketamine was administered intravenously for 1 minute for patients in the esketamine group; the same volume of normal saline was injected for patients in the control group.

Data Collection and Outcome Assessment

Baseline data included demographic characteristics, pregestational comorbidity, number of gravidity and parturitions, duration of gestation, and ASA classification. Intraoperative data included durations of anesthesia and surgery, fluid balance, use of vasopressors, intervals from study drug administration and uterine incision to delivery, and neonatal data.

Our coprimary outcomes included maternal pain intensity and sedation score immediately after fetal delivery. Pain intensity was assessed with the numeric rating scale (NRS) at eight time points from before anesthesia to 12 hours after surgery; a change of 1.65 points or more was considered clinically meaningful. Maternal sedation level was assessed with the Ramsay Sedation Scale at the same time points, and a change of 2 points or more was considered clinically meaningful.

3

Our secondary outcomes included maternal pain intensity and sedation score at other time points, neonatal Apgar score assessed at 1 and 5 minutes after birth, and postnatal umbilical vein blood gas pH value. After childbirth, samples of maternal arterial blood, neonatal umbilical arterial blood, and umbilical venous blood were collected in the esketamine group (n = 13) and stored in a −20 °C refrigerator. Esketamine concentrations were measured with reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography.

Adverse events were monitored. Hypotension was defined as a systolic blood pressure decrease less than 20% of baseline, hypertension as an increase greater than 20% of baseline, bradycardia as a heart rate less than 60 beats per minute, tachycardia as greater than 100 beats per minute, and desaturation as oxygen saturation less than 90%. We also monitored the occurrence of nausea and vomiting, as well as neurologic and mental signs and symptoms (such as lethargy, diplopia, nystagmus, dizziness, headache, nightmare, hallucination, anxiety, and irritability).

Statistical Analysis

On the basis of our pilot study, we hypothesized that the mean difference in NRS pain score immediately after fetal delivery would be 0.3, with SDs of 0.85 in the esketamine group and 1.23 in the control group. With α set at .05 and power at 90%, 263 patients were needed in each group to detect a significant difference. Considering a dropout rate of approximately 10%, we planned to enroll 300 patients in each group. The sample size was estimated with the PASS software, version 15.0 (NCSS).

Outcome analysis was performed in the intention-to-treat population; that is, all patients were analyzed in the group to which they were assigned. For the primary outcomes, we also performed analysis in the per-protocol population, excluding those with protocol deviation.

For continuous variables, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was applied to evaluate the distribution. Variables with normal distribution were presented as means (SDs) and compared using the 2-tailed, unpaired t test. Variables with nonnormal distribution were presented as medians (IQRs) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test and are presented as number (percentage).

For the primary outcomes, the differences between groups were expressed as median differences (95% CIs). For the primary outcomes, a 2-sided P < .025 (.05/2) was considered statistically significant. For other outcomes, a 2-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise indicated after Bonferroni correction. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corp) and GraphPad Prism, version 5.0 (GraphPad Software Inc).

Results

Patients

From 903 parturients who were assessed for eligibility, a total of 600 (mean [SD] age, 30.7 [4.3] years; mean [SD] prenatal body mass index, 25.9 [2.1]; mean [SD] gestational duration, 38.7 [1.1] weeks) were enrolled and randomized. During the study period, 3 women did not receive study drugs, and 4 women refused follow-up at 12 hours after the surgery. As a result, all 600 women (n = 300 in each group) were included in the intention-to-treat analysis, and 597 women were included in the per-protocol analysis (Figure 1). Baseline and intraoperative data, including the intervals from study drug administration to delivery, were comparable between the 2 groups (Table 1).

Efficacy Outcomes

Immediately after fetal delivery, the NRS pain score was lower in the esketamine group (median [IQR], 0 [0-1]) than in the placebo group (median [IQR], 0 [0-2]; median difference, 0; 95% CI, 0-0; P = .001); however, the difference was not clinically meaningful (Figure 2A). The Ramsay Sedation Score was higher (sedation deeper) in the esketamine group (median [IQR], 4 [3-4]) than in the placebo group (median [IQR], 2 [2-2]; median difference, 2; 95% CI, 2-2; P < .001); the difference was clinically important (Figure 2B). Per-protocol analysis gave similar results.

Among secondary and other outcomes, the NRS pain score at other time points did not differ between groups. The Ramsay Sedation Scores were higher in the esketamine group at surgical incision (median difference, 1; 95% CI, 1-1; P < .001) and at 5 minutes after study drug administration (median difference, 2; 95% CI, 2-2; P < .001). The neonatal Apgar scores at 1 and 5 minutes, the umbilical vein blood pH, and hospital stay after surgery did not differ between the 2 groups. No neonatal ward admission was required in either group.

In an exploratory analysis, the proportion of patients with an NRS pain score greater than 2 immediately after fetal delivery was significantly lower in patients given esketamine (6.0% [18 of 300] vs 10.7% [32 of 300]; RR, 0.56; 95% CI, 0.32-0.98; P = .04); the proportion with Ramsay Sedation Scale scores of 4 or higher was significantly higher (71.7% [215 of 300] vs 0%; RR, 431.00; 95% CI, 27.00-6881.35).

After childbirth, the mean (SD) plasma concentrations of esketamine were 172.4 (79.8) ng/mL in maternal arterial blood, 62.0 (23.6) ng/mL in neonatal umbilical arterial blood, and 142.9 (66.7) ng/mL in umbilical venous blood. The mean (SD) ratio of fetal umbilical vein to maternal artery was 0.83 (0.09).

Safety Outcomes

During the intraoperative period, more patients given esketamine developed neurologic or mental symptoms (97.7% [293 of 300]) compared with the placebo group (4.7% [14 of 300]; P < .001), mainly somnolence (70.7% vs 0.0%), nystagmus (67.3% vs 0.0%), vertigo (44.7% vs 0.3%), dizziness (15.0% vs 4.0%), hypertonia (7.7% vs 0.0%), and daymare (1.3% vs 0.0%).

Among other adverse events, fewer patients given esketamine developed hypotension (4.7% [14 of 300]) compared with the placebo group (8.7% [26 of 300]; P = .047), but more developed hypertension (7.7% vs 1.3%; P < .001) and tachycardia (27.0% vs 16.7%; P = .002). During the first postoperative day, more patients given esketamine developed nausea or vomiting (23.0% [69 of 300]) compared with the placebo group (11.0% [33 of 300]; P < .001), but mental symptoms did not differ between the groups.

Discussion

Results of this multicenter randomized clinical trial showed that, for women undergoing elective cesarean delivery with epidural anesthesia, a single subanesthetic dose of esketamine administered before incision transiently relieved pain intensity, but the effect was not clinically important; in the meantime, it produced transient but clinically important sedation. A significant part of esketamine transferred across the placenta to the fetus, but the neonatal Apgar score was not affected. Use of esketamine significantly increased neurologic and mental symptoms, but the adverse effects were transient and only appeared during the intraoperative period.

Neuraxial anesthesia, including epidural anesthesia, is the primary choice for cesarean delivery, but supplemental analgesics are often required to relieve visceral traction pain during surgery. Ketamine is a frequently used analgesic for this purpose. However, the potential effects of neonatal depression and even neurotoxic effects, as well as sympathetic excitation and psychiatric symptoms, raised concern when using ketamine. Esketamine is more potent in analgesia and less likely to produce psychiatric adverse effects; thus, esketamine might be a suitable substitute for women undergoing cesarean delivery.

Limitations

This study has limitations. Plasma esketamine concentrations were measured in only 13 participants, follow-up was limited to the first postoperative day, and long-term maternal and neonatal outcomes were not assessed. Participants were recruited from 5 centers in a single country, which may limit generalisability.

Conclusions

In this randomized clinical trial, a subanesthetic dose of esketamine given before incision produced transient analgesia and clinically important sedation without significant neonatal depression. Indications and the optimal dose of esketamine in this patient population need further clarification, but use should be limited to those who require supplemental analgesia.
