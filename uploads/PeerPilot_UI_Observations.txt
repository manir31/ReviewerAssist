## Methods: Study design
Two secondary outcomes not prespecified in trial registry
The registry lists secondary outcomes including maternal pain and sedation at other timepoints, hemodynamic
variables, Apgar scores, umbilical vein pH, and esketamine levels, but does not include neonatal ward admission or
length of postoperative hospital stay. The manuscript analyzes both of these as predefined outcomes (Table 2 and
text). Adding unregistered secondary outcomes without clear disclosure introduces risk of selective emphasis,
especially for safety/health service utilization endpoints. Although they are not primary, this discrepancy weakens
alignment between protocol and reporting and is therefore rated as a major registration issue.
Recommendation:
Explicitly state in the Methods that neonatal ward admission and postoperative length of stay were added after
initial registration, including when and why. Update the trial registry to include these outcomes, and clearly
distinguish prespecified from post hoc endpoints in the manuscript.

## Methods: Study design
Completion date differs substantially from registry record
The manuscript reports enrollment from September 18, 2021, to September 20, 2022, implying that both primary and
follow-up data collection were completed by 2022. The public registry lists an overall completion date of 2024-09-30.
This ~2-year discrepancy is not explained (e.g., earlier-than-planned completion, extended follow-up, or registry not
updated). While it may be administrative, such inconsistencies complicate external verification and can obscure
whether additional data were collected beyond what is analyzed here. Because transparency about trial status and
completion is central to trust, this is a major, but remediable, weakness.
Recommendation:
Clarify in the manuscript whether the trial finished earlier than the registry completion date or whether this report
reflects a prespecified interim or primary-completion analysis. Update the registry record to match the actual
completion date and describe any differences between registry and manuscript timelines.

## Methods: Study design
Power calculation targets difference below stated clinically important change
The sample size was powered to detect a mean difference of 0.3 points on the 0–10 NRS (SD 0.85–1.23) with 90%
power, yet the Methods define a minimal clinically meaningful change as ≥1.65 NRS points. Thus, the trial was explicitly
powered to detect a statistically significant effect much smaller than the prespecified threshold of clinical importance.
This creates a design mismatch: the study may detect trivial differences while being underpowered for the effect size
considered clinically meaningful. The authors partly mitigate this by appropriately concluding that the statistically
significant difference in pain was not clinically important, but the initial power specification remains suboptimal.
"0.3, with SDs of 0.85 in the esketamine group and 1.23 in the control group.
Recommendation:
Align future power calculations with the predefined minimal clinically important difference (e.g., 1.65 NRS points), or
justify in the protocol why targeting a smaller difference is clinically meaningful. In the current paper, add a brief
comment acknowledging that the power calculation was based on a smaller effect than the clinical threshold.

## Methods: Study design
Extensive exploratory and safety comparisons without broad multiplicity control
The study conducts numerous comparisons: multiple timepoints for pain and sedation, several hemodynamic
measurements, and a long list of adverse events, plus additional exploratory dichotomizations (e.g., NRS>2, Ramsay≥4)
that were not clearly prespecified. Bonferroni adjustments are applied for some repeated measures (eTable 2, eTable
3), but no comprehensive strategy is described for the many safety and exploratory endpoints. This increases the risk
of inflated type I error, especially for modest associations. Large, highly significant differences (e.g., neurologic/mental
symptoms 97.7% vs 4.7%, p<.001) are robust, but smaller p≈0.04–0.05 findings may be less reliable.
"proportion of patients with Ramsay Sedation Scale scores of 4 or higher ... were significantly higher
Recommendation:
Clearly distinguish prespecified from exploratory analyses, especially for dichotomized outcomes. Consider
applying or at least discussing appropriate multiplicity control for families of safety and exploratory endpoints, and
temper conclusions about marginal p-values that may be susceptible to chance findings.

## Methods: Study design
Placental transfer substudy small and potentially unrepresentative
Maternal arterial, umbilical arterial, and umbilical venous esketamine concentrations were measured in only 13 women
in the esketamine group. The sampling strategy for these 13 (e.g., consecutive, convenience, or selected) is not
described, raising potential selection bias. While these data are appropriately interpreted as exploratory, the small,
possibly non-representative subsample limits the precision and generalizability of conclusions about placental transfer
kinetics.
"concentrations were measured
Recommendation:
Clarify how the 13 participants for pharmacokinetic sampling were selected and emphasize more strongly in the
Results and Discussion that these findings are exploratory and may not fully represent the whole esketamine group.
If feasible in future work, plan a larger, systematically sampled PK substudy.

## Methods: Study design
Prospective trial registration consistent with primary outcomes and sample size
The trial was prospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04548973) on 2020-09-16, before first enrollment on
2021-09-18. The registered primary outcomes (maternal pain intensity and maternal sedation level) match the
manuscript's coprimary outcomes in construct, and the planned enrollment of 600 participants matches the reported
randomized sample. This supports transparency of the primary objectives and minimizes risk of primary outcome
switching or selective reporting. The availability of a formal protocol and statistical analysis plan as Supplement 1
further strengthens credibility.

## Methods: Study design
Robust multicenter randomization with allocation concealment and blinding
The study uses a multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled parallel-group design with 1:1 randomization, central
generation of the sequence in SPSS, and sequentially numbered opaque envelopes for concealment. Study
coordinators uninvolved in outcome assessment prepared indistinguishable esketamine and saline syringes, and all
patients, anesthesiologists, and outcome assessors were blinded. Such procedures minimize selection and
performance bias and are fully appropriate for the research question about efficacy and safety of esketamine as
supplemental analgesia.
"Assignments were concealed in sequentially numbered opaque envelopes.

## Methods: Study design
Clear eligibility criteria and clinically relevant target population
Inclusion and exclusion criteria are explicit and clinically sensible: adult women with term singleton pregnancies
scheduled for elective cesarean delivery under epidural anesthesia, excluding significant comorbidities and obstetric
complications. This yields a relatively homogeneous, ASA II population suitable for evaluating sedative and analgesic
effects and neonatal safety. The multicenter design with fixed per-site quotas (Table 1, eTable 1) supports external
validity within similar tertiary hospital settings.
"examination and were scheduled for elective cesarean delivery with epidural anesthesia.

## Methods: Study design
Appropriate analyses with ITT, per-protocol, and multiplicity control
The authors pre-specify an intention-to-treat primary analysis with a supportive per-protocol analysis for coprimary
outcomes, which is good practice in RCTs. They use Mann-Whitney U tests for NRS pain and Ramsay Sedation scores,
which are ordinal and skewed, and t tests or chi-square tests where appropriate. For the two coprimary outcomes they
adjust alpha to 0.025 (0.05/2), and for repeated timepoints they apply Bonferroni corrections (e.g., p<0.006 for 8 pain
/sedation timepoints). Effect estimates are reported with 95% CIs (median differences, RR, mean differences, HR),
facilitating interpretation beyond p-values. Overall, the statistical methods are appropriate and clearly reported.
"were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test.

## Methods: Study design
CONSORT-style reporting with minimal attrition and clear flow
The manuscript describes screening, randomization, exclusions, and follow-up, with a flow diagram (Figure 1) and
explicit counts of deviations (e.g., 3 not dosed, 4 missing 12-hour follow-up). All 600 randomized women are included
in the intention-to-treat analysis, and 597 in per-protocol analyses. Baseline characteristics and intraoperative variables
by group are presented (Table 1), showing good balance. This level of reporting supports internal validity and allows
readers to assess potential attrition or selection biases, which appear minimal.
During the study period, 3 women did not receive study drugs, and 4 women refused follow-up at 12 hours after the
"surgery.

## Methods: Study design
Site enrollment totals internally consistent with overall sample size
A numerical consistency check of eTable 1 confirms that the number of participants enrolled at each site (180, 160, 80,
120, 60) sums exactly to the total sample size of 600 reported in the main text. This internal consistency supports the
reliability of the recruitment data and suggests careful data management across participating centers.

## Methods: Study design
Adverse event percentages accurately calculated from denominators
Recalculation of key adverse event percentages, such as hypotension (26/300=8.7%), hypertension (23/300=7.7%),
tachycardia (81/300=27.0%), and postoperative nausea/vomiting (69/300=23.0%), shows that all reported values are
correct within rounding tolerance. Accurate reporting of event rates enhances the trustworthiness of the safety profile
and suggests careful tabulation of outcomes.

## Methods: Study design
Reference list contains duplicate and unverified entries
Integrity Checks identify 4 duplicate references (e.g., the Strümper et al. 2004 sheep study and the Suppa et al. 2012
esketamine infusion trial each listed twice with slightly different formatting) and 2 unverified references, one of which is
an incomplete entry. With 56 total citations, this represents approximately 7% duplication plus some bibliographic
incompleteness. While these issues do not change the substantive evidence base, they reflect imperfect reference
management and can complicate verification by readers and indexers. From a literature‐use perspective, duplicated
entries may subtly inflate apparent citation volume and obscure how many distinct sources underlie key statements,
which conflicts with best‐practice reporting standards (e.g., ICMJE).
Recommendation:
Clean the reference list to remove duplicates, correct incomplete entries, and ensure that each numbered reference
corresponds to a single, fully verifiable source.
